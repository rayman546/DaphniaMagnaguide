Daphnia Magna Culturing Guide

From Nano Jars to Stock Tanks: The Complete Live Food Blueprint.

Beginner Expert Mode

Container Scale & Aeration

Daphnia can be cultured in almost any clean vessel that holds water, allowing you to tailor your setup to your space and feeding requirements.

1. Nano Setups (< 4 Liters)

Ideal for: Casual feeding for a single nano tank, breeding fry.

Use large glass jars or repurposed transparent plastic bottles. They do not require an air pump if placed in a brightly lit area to encourage gentle algae growth.

Expert Micro-cultures are highly susceptible to sudden crashes due to trace ammonia spikes. Their low thermal mass makes them vulnerable to rapid temperature fluctuations. Daily monitoring and a rigid feeding schedule are mandatory.

2. Medium setups (10 - 40 Liters)

Ideal for: Regularly feeding several community tanks.

Plastic storage totes or empty small aquariums work beautifully. Utilize a slow-bubbling airline or a gentle sponge filter to keep the water from stagnating.

3. Large Stock Tanks (50 - 200+ Liters)

Ideal for: Heavy feeding, breeders, fishrooms.

Outdoor or indoor masonry tubs and large garbage cans. These setups are the most stable. In outdoor settings, naturally occurring algae and seasonal temperature shifts dictate the reproductive cycles.

Expert High-volume outdoor cultures benefit immensely from the increased surface-area-to-volume ratio. However, beware of unwanted macro-predators like dragonfly nymphs. To mitigate gas entrapment under the D. magna carapace, use open rigid airline tubing rather than fine-pore airstones.

Water Parameters & Environment

Daphnia magna are sensitive to their environment. Treating them with the same care as fish ensures maximum yield.

  • Temperature: 18°C – 22°C is optimal. Avoid placing containers in direct, harsh sunlight that may overheat the water.
  • pH: 7.0 – 8.5. They prefer slightly alkaline water.
  • Water Source: Dechlorinated tap water or aged tank water is mandatory. Chlorine kills them instantly.
Expert Deep Dive

Thermal Stress & Hemoglobin: When temperatures exceed 25°C, oxygen saturation drops. In response, D. magna produce hemoglobin, causing the culture to turn a distinct red or pink color. This is a severe warning sign of impending anoxia.

Water Hardness: Maintain 90–180 mg/L CaCO3. Soft water causes failed molting (ecdysozoan shedding issues). Adding crushed coral or a small amount of Plaster of Paris can buffer osmotic stress in soft-water regions.

Water Changes

Replace 10–25% of the culture water weekly. Siphon the bottom carefully to remove waste (mulm) and replace it with slow-dripped, fresh dechlorinated water.

Feeding Your Daphnia

Daphnia are filter feeders. They consume microscopic particles suspended in the water column. The Golden Rule: Never feed if the water is still cloudy from the previous feeding.

Food Types

  • Green Water (Microalgae): The absolute best and most natural food. If you can grow green water in a separate container in the sun, feed it heavily. It will not foul the water easily.
  • Spirulina Powder: Highly nutritious, but very dense. Because it is dead organic matter, bacteria will rapidly decompose excess powder, driving an ammonia spike. Mix the powder into a small jar of water and shake it vigorously to create a "slurry" before dosing.
  • Active Dry Yeast: Cheap and easily accessible. Creates an immediate bloom of bacteria for the Daphnia to eat. Must be used very sparingly.
Expert Nutritional Enrichment Yeast alone is severely deficient in Highly Unsaturated Fatty Acids (HUFAs) and Essential Amino Acids. If feeding primarily yeast, you must "gut load" the Daphnia with an enrichment formula, astaxanthin powder, or emulsified fish oils 2-4 hours prior to feeding them to your fish to transfer proper nutrition.

Harvesting & Tank Hygiene

Harvesting prevents overcrowding, which prompts population stagnation.

How to Harvest

Use a fine-mesh brine shrimp net. Scoop gently near the surface or the side of the container where they congregate toward the light. Alternatively, use a siphon to drain water through a sieve—this doubles as a water change!

How Often?

Aim to harvest up to 25% of the visible population every 3 to 4 days. This keeps the population predominantly young and deeply focused on reproduction rather than resource competition.

Expert High-density populations limit their own fecundity via chemical signaling and physical crowding. Harvesting breaks this loop. Furthermore, always rinse the harvested Daphnia in clean, dechlorinated water before introducing them to your fish tank to avoid cross-contaminating your aquarium with ammonia-heavy culture water.

Troubleshooting Diagnostic Tree

Cultures invariably crash if mistreated. Select the symptom that best matches your situation to identify the cause.

Expert Recovery Tactics If a total wipeout occurs, do not throw the detritus away immediately. Stressed Daphnia produce Ephippia (dark, saddle-shaped, dormant resting cysts). Siphon away the fouled water, gently replace it with fresh, cool water, and wait. Ephippia can hatch weeks or even months later, rebooting your culture automatically.
